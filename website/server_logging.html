Server Logging

Introduction

There's always been this narrative that companies and websites are stealing our data. It's gotten so prevalent that we now have laws requiring us to accept or decline cookies, as well as trillion dollar companies having to testify to congress about what data they hold and what they're doing with it.
As I've gotten deeper into computer science, web development, and data engineering, I've come to understand what information you're actually giving away when you visit a website even when you reject all cookies! The purpose of this page is to break down what data you're sharing regardless of which cookies you accept, why websites need this data, and what could go wrong if it falls into the wrong hands.

What Data Do Websites Need to Function?

Every single time you visit a website there is an exchange of information. The website needs to identify your computer, know what you want to do, and then send files back to your browser (usually HTML and CSS). The data your browser has to send to the website is called a request, and the server records these as request logs. An example of what one looks like is shown below: 
{
    "method": "GET",
    "path": "/differentiation.html",
    "ip": "203.0.113.42",
    "headers": {
        "host": "computermath.com",
        "user-agent": "Mozilla/5.0 (Windows NT 10.0; Win64; x64) AppleWebKit/537.36 (KHTML, like Gecko) Chrome/124.0.0.0 Safari/537.36",
        "accept": "text/html,application/xhtml+xml,application/xml;q=0.9,image/webp,*/*;q=0.8",
        "accept-language": "en-GB,en;q=0.9",
        "accept-encoding": "gzip, deflate, br",
        "connection": "keep-alive"
    },
    "query_params": {},
    "timestamp": "2026-06-03T10:23:41+00:00"
}
What we get here is a method (what the user wants from our webserver), and the path, which is the file the user is trying to get, in this case /differentiation.html. We also have the IP of the computer making the request, which is used to identify who is trying to access your server. On top of that there are a few extra pieces of data, such as the website URL (host), the browser being used (user-agent), and the language the website is loaded in.
So far this is not that crazy. I can't tell who this person is, where they live, or anything personal at all. The main concern would be the IP, which is legally personally identifiable information (PII), where every person has their own unique IP, similar to someone's government number (national insurance number / social security number), except instead of identifying a person like a government ID, it identifies the machine you're on. You can't publicly share the IPs of everyone accessing your website, so I've tried my best to keep any instance of someone's IP hidden on this page. If you happen to find your IP or anyone else's being displayed on this page, please let me know and I'll try to remove it as soon as possible.

Why Do Websites Need This Data?

This data is essential to webservers as it can help ban malicious people from bringing harm to your website. Say a bad actor is accessing your webserver and leaving nasty comments like "you smell." You probably don't want this person getting access to your server files, or scaring other people away. So we want to block them while letting everyone else through. We can do this by IP banning them, where our webserver collects their request and then refuses to serve any of its files, preventing them from being mean to you or anyone else on your website.

What Could Someone Do with Your Server Logs?

So what can someone actually do with this data once they have it? At the very least, they can figure out roughly where you're connecting from, and at worst, combine it with other data to build a much more detailed picture of who you are. Let's break this down.

Visitor Map

First of all, we can get somebody's rough location using their IP. This is because internet service providers (ISPs) are assigned IP blocks by regional registries, and this is all public information. Here is a map showing the location of the last few people to visit this page:
This is a little bit scary, but the locations are very rough. These locations are somewhat accurate at saying what city you may be from, but when it comes to your actual house or your precise location, it's impossible to know just from your IP. Still, it is valuable data, and a simple example of how it gets used is serving personalised ads based on location.
But is it possible to get more personal information about an individual from their IP? What would you need to do, and is it even legal?

Scary Databases

The way for someone to find out your actual address or get your name from your IP is if they already have your IP stored alongside some personal information about you, like your address and name. You can't just get any of this from your IP alone. But there are companies that already have this information. Think of any website you've filled in a form with your name, address, or other details (social media, bank accounts, your internet service provider). Did you fill that in on a webpage at some point? Well, that company now has a database with your name, address, and IP. Most of the time these services are pretty trustworthy, and this is honestly fine. The scary part is if they have a data leak, because then bad actors can take that database, linking IPs to exact locations and names, and use it on you without ever having to ask you for anything (theoretically).

Conclusion

So, should you be worried every time you load a webpage? Not really. On its own, a server log is just a handful of technical details that a website needs to actually work, and on this page that's roughly a country or city, a browser, and a timestamp. The bigger risk isn't any single website logging your visit, it's what happens when that data is combined with other databases that already know who you are, and what happens if those databases get leaked. That's why, as developers, it's worth treating IPs and other identifying details with the same care as passwords: collect only what you need, store it securely, and avoid publishing it anywhere it doesn't need to be. Hopefully this page has given you a slightly clearer picture of what's actually being shared every time you click a link, and why the people building these systems take that responsibility seriously.
